# V7.2.0 — GPU for large-scale SRAM: which half do we ship to the GPU?

**Status:** plan only, no behaviour change. Branch `v720-gpu-scaling-plan`.
**Revision 4 (2026-07-27)** — re-targeted to the workload that actually
matters: **large-scale SRAM *transient* simulation** (read/write `.tran`,
then transient Monte Carlo), **not `.op`/DC**. Revisions 1–3 measured,
budgeted and routed almost entirely on `.op`; those measurements stand below
as the per-NR-iteration cost model, but the routing built on them does not
survive the workload change. A first *measured* write transient
(`scripts/bench_gpu/prof_tran.py`) also exposed a cost class every previous
revision missed entirely. §11 lists the rev-4 retractions.

> ### ⚠ The headline changed again in revision 4 — the workload is `.tran`
>
> Three facts, from the first *measured* SRAM write transient (4×4, 200 steps,
> production two-stage path):
>
> 1. **A cost class no prior revision modelled dominates the transient: the
>    post-step charge-history commit.** After every accepted timestep the
>    solver calls `get_charges(timestep_voltages)` per device
>    (`solver.py:2238`, then again via `update_charge_state`) at the
>    just-solved voltages — which the NR-loop batch eval never evaluated. So
>    **every device runs a cache-cold, batch-1 forward + 3 backwards, every
>    accepted step**: 200 steps × 96 devices = 19,296 single-row evals —
>    **75 % of the profiled transient wall (83 % by counter split; 82 % at
>    8×8)**. The call-count arithmetic is exact: *every* NR-loop `_eval` hits
>    the warm batch cache; *every* commit-path `_eval` misses. **[M, §2.4t]**
>    At 32×32 this term alone is **13–22 s per timestep**; at 256×128,
>    ~7–12 min per timestep — the transient plan is unusable until batched.
> 2. **NR iterations per accepted step measured 2.42, not the 4 assumed** by
>    rev 3's §2.4 [A] — identical at 4×4 and 8×8; the LM ladder *does* fire
>    in transient (+28 % solves at both sizes). **[M]**
> 3. **Parse amortises and is demoted.** 3 s of parse against 94 s of
>    transient at a *toy* 4×4; at real sizes and step counts parse is 1–30 %,
>    not the 83–85 % of the `.op` budget. Rev 3's "parse first" headline was
>    an `.op` artefact — though parse returns as a ~30 % term at the very end
>    of the fix ladder, and parse-once still gates MC. **[M/E, §2.6t]**
>
> The rev-3 `.op` ranking (parse 83 % → per-device Python → solver ordering →
> GPU) is **retained for `.op`** but no longer routes the plan. The transient
> ranking is: **batch the commit path → GPU NN eval (it carries the NR loop
> *and* the batched commit) → vectorise per-device Python → solver ordering +
> refactorisation → parse**.

> #### The superseded rev-3 headline (`.op`-only, kept as the `.op` budget)
>
> At **256×128** (196,608 devices), one `.op`:
>
> | term | today | after this plan | share after |
> | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
> | **parse** | 24 min | **9.8 min** | **83 %** |
> | per-device Python (`other` + stamp) | 7.2 min | 1.6 min | 14 % |
> | linear solver | 23 min | 14.4 s | 2 % |
> | **NN forward+Jacobian (the GPU target)** | ~2 h | **1.4 s** | **0.2 %** |
> | **total, one `.op`** | **~3 hours** | **~12 min** | |
>
> True for `.op`; `.op` is not the workload. **[M/E, §2.5]**

**Evidence tags.** Every load-bearing claim carries one:
**[M]** measured here, script cited · **[E]** estimated from measured inputs,
arithmetic shown · **[A]** assumed, not yet verified.
Untagged prose is argument, not evidence.

**The question.** For large-scale SRAM **transient** work, do we ship the
**NN compact model** to the GPU, or the **circuit solver**? This document
answers it with a measured scaling profile rather than intuition, and routes
the work that follows.

**The answer, up front:**

> **Ship the NN to the GPU — and in a transient it is decisive, not
> incidental, because every NN cost is multiplied by 10³–10⁵ NR iterations
> instead of 41. Still do not ship the *solver* to the GPU: fix its ordering
> and stop re-factorising from scratch 10⁵ times.** Ranked by payoff on the
> transient workload (§2.4t, §2.6t):
>
> 1. **Batch the post-step charge-history commit** — one batched eval per
>    accepted step instead of N cache-cold batch-1 evals. **75–83 % of the
>    measured transient wall today**; 13–22 s *per timestep* at 32×32. The
>    single biggest lever in the plan, ~10 lines, perturbing (single-row →
>    batched rows), default-off. §2.4t, Phase 2t.
> 2. **The GPU NN eval** — carries both the NR-loop batch *and* the batched
>    commit: at 6144 devices, 611 ms → 2.6 ms per transient iteration,
>    movement-inclusive. On this workload the GPU is second, not fifth. §3.
> 3. **Vectorise the per-device Python** (`other`, stamping incl. the
>    transcap block, denorm tail) — per-NR-iteration cost × 483 iterations
>    per 200-step op, not × 41. Worth ~6.6× on the NR loop alone, and it is
>    what remains binding *after* the GPU lands (§2.6t). §7.
> 4. **The solver: `MMD_AT_PLUS_A` ordering now, refactorisation next.** A
>    200-step transient factorises ~620 times, a 10⁴-step one ~3×10⁴ times —
>    the `splu` solve-only step is 30–600× cheaper than a fresh factorise, so
>    KLU-class refactorisation is **partially re-promoted** for ≥64×32
>    transients (rev 3 had downgraded it on `.op` arithmetic). §5.2, Phase 4b.
> 5. **Parse** — demoted for Track A (it amortises over the transient), still
>    first-class for MC (parse-once, §9) and back to ~30 % once everything
>    above lands. §6.
>
> **The bit-identical subset is still the best payoff-to-risk item** — Phases
> 1 and 2, no GPU, no re-gate — but on the transient its value is ~6.6× on
> the NR loop (not the 1.71× end-to-end that `.op` arithmetic suggested),
> because parse no longer caps it. **[E, §2.4]**
>
> The GPU buys nothing on the **unmodified** path — a statement about **one
> line** (`.tolist()` on a CUDA slice, once per device), not about GPUs; a
> ~10-line D2H move fixes it. **[M, §3.3, §7]** The commit-path finding is
> the same disease in a second location: the transient path evaluates
> devices one at a time in exactly the place where the array is largest.
>
> **On data movement: the PCIe bus is never the bottleneck — the Python
> marshalling around it is.** Measured end-to-end with full synchronisation, one
> NR iteration at 6144 devices costs **1.57 ms** done correctly against
> **12.02 ms** the way the code writes it today — a **7.7× penalty**, rising to
> **22×** at 24576 devices. **[M, §3.4]** The irreducible part is a **56.8 µs**
> round trip per NR iteration, which is negligible for a large array but
> **~10× the work for a single cell** — so small circuits can only reach the
> GPU by batching across *circuits*, i.e. Track B.

## 0. Goal, scope, and success criteria

The previous draft never stated what it was optimising *for*, which left every
speedup number unanchored. Fixing that first.

**What the SRAM simulations are for.** Two workloads, both in scope — and
**both are transient workloads** (rev 4). Read/write margin, write timing and
read disturb are `.tran` measurements; `.op` appears only as the transient's
stage-1 initial condition:

* **Track A — array-level characterisation.** One large array, one MNA system.
  Read/write **transients** and array-level timing on the full size range.
* **Track B — Monte Carlo / yield.** ≥100 **transient** re-runs of a full
  array with per-device mismatch. The deliverable is a **distribution tail**,
  not a mean.

**The canonical unit of work (rev 4).** One **write op**: wordline pulse +
bitline drive, 10 ns window at 50 ps steps = **200 accepted timesteps**, ~2.4
NR iterations per step (measured, §2.4t) ≈ **483 NR iterations + 200 commit
points** — versus the 41 NR iterations of the `.op` every previous revision
budgeted on. Longer characterisation runs (10⁴ steps) scale linearly ×50.

**Target sizes — confirmed by the requester, not assumed.**

| | array | cells | **devices** | MNA nodes |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| smallest | **32×16** | 512 | **3,072** | 1,154 |
| largest | **256×128** | 32,768 | **196,608** | 66,562 |

Plus **≥100 Monte Carlo runs at the array level** — each sample re-simulates a
*whole array* with per-device Vt/geometry mismatch. (Not thousands of isolated
cells; see §9, which is rewritten accordingly.)

Note the span: the largest target is **32× more devices than anything
measured in revisions 1–2**, and 64× the smallest target. No single bottleneck
holds across that range, which is why §2.5 tabulates the regime by size.

**Success criteria (rev 4 — transient).** End-to-end wall clock, parse
included, for one 200-step write op (§2.6t arithmetic; today-columns are [E]
from measured per-iteration and per-eval costs, anchored by the measured 4×4):

| workload | today **[E]** | target | reachable? |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 32×16 write op (3,072 dev) | ~45 min | < 5 min | yes — ~3.5 min CPU-only, ~25 s with GPU **[E]** |
| 32×32 write op (6,144 dev) | ~88 min | < 10 min | yes — ~8 min CPU-only, ~1 min with GPU **[E]** |
| 128×64 write op (49,152 dev) | ~12 h | < 30 min | yes — ~8 min with GPU **[E]** |
| **256×128 write op** (196,608 dev) | **~2.2 days** | **< 1 h** | yes — **~28 min** with GPU **[E]** |
| **100-sample transient MC @ 256×128** | **months (unusable)** | ~overnight | **~10 h** on 3 GPUs, *if parse is amortised* **[E]** |
| *(reference)* 256×128 `.op` | ~3 h | < 20 min | 12 min — the rev-3 result, unchanged **[E]** |

**Two structural gates sit in front of these numbers:** the commit-path batch
fix (§2.4t — without it the 256×128 transient spends ~40 h in cache-cold
batch-1 evals alone) and parse-once for MC (§9). Everything else is
multiplicative refinement.

**Non-goals.** Single cells and small circuits. §3.4's latency floor makes the
GPU *worse* for them, and they are already fast. Every GPU path ships
default-off, so existing small-circuit users are unaffected by construction.

**Out of scope for this plan.** KLU *implementation* (its own investigation,
§7 Phase 4b — re-promoted for transient work in rev 4, but still gated on
4a′'s real-matrix measurement); multi-GPU for a single array (§3.4, rejected
on measurement).

## 1. Measurement protocol (movement-inclusive)

The user requirement that motivated this revision: **reported runtime must
include all data-movement expense.** The previous draft mixed component
microbenchmarks with in-situ profiles and reported some GPU numbers that
excluded transfers. These rules are binding on every number in this document
and every number produced by the work it routes.

**M-1 — The headline metric is end-to-end wall clock of the whole run**, parse
included, process start to results written. An unqualified "speedup" means
this. Solve-only and kernel-only figures may appear only as *named sub-metrics*,
always printed next to the end-to-end figure.

**M-2 — No compute-only GPU numbers.** Every GPU timing brackets the full
sequence — build input from host state → H2D → forward + autograd → D2H →
host-side consumption — with `torch.cuda.synchronize()` immediately before the
start timestamp *and* immediately before the end timestamp. CUDA calls are
async; a number taken without both syncs silently excludes the transfer and is
void.

**M-3 — Median, not mean; report the spread.** ≥5 warm-up iterations discarded,
then ≥30 timed reps; report median and (p10, p90). *This rule exists because of
a specific defect:* `scripts/bench_gpu/bench_nn_batch.py`'s `timeit()` takes the
**mean** over reps after 3 warm-ups. On this shared 192-core box that inflated
the previous draft's CPU column by **4–5×** — §3.2b claimed 344 µs/device at
N=6144 where median-of-30 measures **85.4 µs/device**. **[M]**

**M-4 — Contention discipline.** The three GPUs are shared with other users.
Every GPU measurement records `utilization.gpu` and `memory.used` at start and
end; any run whose device showed >5 % foreign utilisation is tagged
`[contended]` and **may not be used for a headline number**. CPU measurements
record 1-minute loadavg. All GPU numbers in this revision were taken on GPU 2
while it was idle (0 %, 1 MiB).

**M-5 — Baseline definition.** The CPU baseline is the repo gate configuration:
`CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES="" OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 MKL_NUM_THREADS=1`, torch pinned to
one thread. Comparing against a multi-threaded CPU run is not permitted —
thread count is a documented gate-flip axis here.

**M-6 — Report template.** Every performance claim carries: `N devices |
analysis | end-to-end s | parse s | solve s | NN tensor s | tail s | stamp s |
spsolve s | device | contention flag | median of R reps`.

**M-7 — Acceptance criteria are stated end-to-end**, not as component ratios.

**M-8 — Reproducibility.** Scripts are committed at **`scripts/bench_gpu/`**
(the previous draft wrongly said "the session scratchpad"). Every table cites
its script and command line.

## 2. Where the time actually goes

6T SRAM array, R×C cells, generated hierarchically (`.subckt sraminv` + access
pair), DirectNet `large` (907,565 params) TSMC5 N/P, `.op` with `.ic` on every
cell. Host: 192-core CPU, 3× RTX 4090. Node count is `2·cells + rows + 2·cols + 1`.

`nn` = `_batch_eval_nn_mosfets`, `stamp` = the per-device `_stamp_mosfet_dc`
loop, `spsolve` = the MNA solve. **[M — `scripts/bench_gpu/prof_scale.py`]**

| array | devices | nodes | **solve** | nn | stamp | spsolve | tocsr | other | **parse** | **end-to-end** |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2×2 | 24 | 15 | 0.24 s | 0.17 | 0.04 | 0.01 | 0.00 | 0.02 | — | — |
| 4×4 | 96 | 45 | 0.49 s | 0.33 | 0.12 | 0.01 | 0.00 | 0.03 | 0.4 s | 0.9 s |
| 8×8 | 384 | 153 | 1.89 s | 1.22 | 0.52 | 0.04 | 0.00 | 0.10 | 2.1 s | 4.0 s |
| 16×16 | 1536 | 561 | 8.25 s | 5.33 | 2.52 | 0.05 | 0.01 | 0.34 | 14.7 s | 23.0 s |
| 32×32 | 6144 | 2145 | **40.38 s** | 26.68 | 11.55 | **0.22** | 0.03 | 1.89 | 44.4 s | **84.8 s** |

> **Disclosure required by M-6.** The `solve` column here and the "solve CPU"
> column in §3.3 are **different configurations** and must not be compared
> across sections: §2 is `prof_scale.py` (profiler attached, `.ic` on every
> cell); §3.3 is `prof_split.py` (finer instrumentation, higher overhead), which
> is why it reports 11.21 s where §2 reports 8.25 s for the same 16×16 array.
> **Neither is wrong; the previous draft simply never said they differ.** Only
> compare within a section. **[M]**

Four facts fall out:

1. **Cost is near-linear in device count, and drifting superlinear.** The number
   of linear solves is *constant* at 41 across all five sizes — the 20-step
   source-stepping schedule at ~2 NR iterations per step, not circuit difficulty
   — so iteration growth is **not** the cause. But the per-step cost exponent
   **rises monotonically**: across the last three ×4 steps the `solve` exponent
   goes 0.98 → 1.06 → **1.15**, and `nn` alone reaches **1.16**. **[M]**

   | step | device ratio | solve ratio | implied exponent |
   | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
   | 96 → 384 | 4.0 | 3.86 | 0.98 |
   | 384 → 1536 | 4.0 | 4.37 | 1.064 |
   | 1536 → 6144 | 4.0 | **4.89** | **1.145** |

   This is per-device cost growing with N — cache pressure in the GEMM, plus the
   `_unpack_eval` tail whose per-call cost grows 19.2 → 32.1 µs over the same
   range (§2.1). **Treat `O(devices)` as a lower bound and re-measure rather
   than extrapolate beyond 6144.** Every extrapolation in this document
   therefore *under*-predicts cost at larger arrays.
   ~~The previous draft claimed "everything is linear in device count", which its
   own table refutes.~~
   **Caveat:** measured on `.op` with `.ic` on every cell, the benign case.
   A read/write transient, or an array without `.ic`, may converge differently
   and the iteration count is then not guaranteed size-independent. **Phase 0
   confirms this.** **[A]**
2. **The linear solve is noise: 0.22 s of 40.38 s = 0.55 %.** Smaller than the
   untracked `other`. **[M]**

   > **Counter caveat — "41" is two different numbers.** `_batch_eval_nn_mosfets`
   > runs once per NR iteration (`solver.py:760`), but `_solve_mna` runs once at
   > `:788` **plus up to 8 more in the Levenberg-Marquardt ladder** at `:811`,
   > which fires only when `_has_nn_device(circuit)` and the residual increased.
   > On an NN circuit the solve count can therefore exceed the NN-eval count by
   > up to 9×. `prof_scale.py` counts both (`n_solve`, `n_batch`) but the plan
   > quoted only one. **Phase 0 must report both separately and state whether LM
   > fired** — if it did, the "41 solves = 20 steps × ~2 NR" story is wrong, and
   > so is every per-iteration extrapolation in §2.3 and §3.4, which assume 41
   > *NR iterations*. **[M]**
3. **Parse costs *more* than the solve** (44.4 s vs 40.4 s at 6144 devices) —
   and it is a bug, not a scaling law. See §6. This is the line item that
   decides whether any of the rest reaches the user.
4. **End-to-end, the solve is only 48 % of the run today**, so every
   solve-only speedup is capped at ~2.1× before parse is touched. **[E]**

### 2.1 Splitting the NN cost: tensor vs Python

`batch_eval` is not all tensor work. Every device's result goes through
`_unpack_eval` — 13 scalar `_denorm` calls, 3–8 `_denorm_deriv` calls,
`_guard_gds`, and the branchy `_apply_vds_correction` — all scalar Python, one
call per device per NR iteration. **[M — `prof_split.py`]**

| array | devices | batch_eval | tensor (GEMM+autograd) | Python tail | µs per unpack |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4×4 | 96 | 0.49 s | 0.41 s (84.5 %) | 0.08 s (15.5 %) | 19.2 |
| 8×8 | 384 | 1.89 s | 1.49 s (78.9 %) | 0.40 s (21.1 %) | 25.4 |
| 16×16 | 1536 | 7.51 s | 5.50 s (73.1 %) | 2.02 s (26.9 %) | 32.1 |

The tail's share **grows** with array size (15.5 → 26.9 %), and so does its
per-call cost (19.2 → 32.1 µs). **Extrapolating the per-call cost — not the
ratio — is the correct move**, and it is what the previous draft got wrong:
applying a ratio you have just shown to be size-dependent, at 4× the size, is
an error of known direction.

| extrapolation method | µs/unpack @6144 | 32×32 tail |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| ~~previous draft (16×16 ratio, no growth)~~ | ~~32.1~~ | ~~7.2 s~~ |
| growth in µs/unpack (×1.26–1.32 per ×4 N) | 40.4–42.4 | **10.2–10.7 s** |
| linear extrapolation of the share (→ ~32.8 %) | ~36.4 | **8.8 s** |

Honest range **8.8–10.7 s**, midpoint **~9.5 s**. Since `batch_eval` total is
measured at 26.68 s, the tensor bucket is the remainder. **[E]**

| bucket | 32×32 time | share of solve | GPU-addressable? |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| NN tensor (forward + autograd) | **17.2 s** | **43 %** | **yes** |
| NN Python tail (`_unpack_eval`) | **9.5 s** | **23 %** | no — vectorise |
| Stamping loop (`_stamp_mosfet_dc`) | 11.6 s | 29 % | no — vectorise |
| `other` (NR bookkeeping, residual) | 1.9 s | 5 % | partly |
| **MNA solve (`spsolve` + `tocsr`)** | **0.25 s** | **0.6 %** | yes, and pointless |
| | **40.45 s** | | (reconciles with §2's 40.38 s to 0.2 %) |

**~52 % of the solve is per-device Python that no GPU can touch, and only
~43 % is GPU-addressable tensor work.** ~~The previous draft said 47 % and 48 %
respectively.~~ The correction makes the case for deleting the Python
*stronger*, not weaker.

### 2.2 The vectorised tail is far better than previously estimated **[M — NEW]**

The previous draft estimated the tail would shrink ~10×. Measured against the
real `_unpack_eval` on the production `tsmc5_dn_large_nmos` checkpoint
(`scripts/bench_gpu/bench_tail.py`), with the bit-exactness constraint of §8
applied:

| N | scalar tail | µs/device | vectorised (bit-exact) | speedup |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 384 | 11.3 ms | 29.4 | 0.35 ms | 32× |
| 1536 | 31.7 ms | 20.7 | 0.71 ms | 45× |
| 6144 | 120.9 ms | 19.7 | 2.07 ms | **58×** |

The tail does not shrink by 10× — it **effectively disappears**. This raises
Phase 2's standalone payoff and is what makes the solve-only figure ~11×
rather than 7–10×.

### 2.3 The end-to-end arithmetic for one `.op` **[E — NEW]** *(rev 4: for the transient workload the operative version of this table is §2.6t)*

Applying the measured component improvements to the corrected 32×32 budget.
The GPU term uses the **movement-inclusive** idle-GPU round trips of §3.2: a 6T
array is 4096 NMOS + 2048 PMOS rows → ~1.40 ms + ~1.25 ms per NR iteration →
**0.11 s** over 41 iterations, replacing 17.2 s of CPU tensor work.

The ladder is split at the line that matters most for this repo — **what ships
default-on without a re-gate, and what needs one**:

| stage | perturbing? | parse | solve | **total** | solve-only | **end-to-end** |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| today | — | 44.4 | 40.4 | **84.8 s** | 1.00× | 1.00× |
| +P1 parse (`torch.load` + `NormStats`) | no | 18.4 | 40.4 | 58.8 s | 1.00× | 1.44× |
| +P2a-lite + 2c (single D2H, cached stack) | no | 18.4 | 40.0 | 58.4 s | 1.01× | 1.45× |
| **+P2a-full (vectorised tail)** | **no** | 18.4 | 31.1 | **49.5 s** | 1.30× | **1.71×** |
| +P3a (GPU NN eval) | **yes** | 18.4 | 14.0 | 32.4 s | 2.89× | 2.62× |
| **+P3b (batched stamping)** | **yes** | 18.4 | **3.6** | **22.0 s** | **11.2×** | **3.85×** |

**Four conclusions the previous draft missed:**

1. **The bit-identical subset alone is worth 1.71× end-to-end** — Phases 1 and
   2, no GPU, no re-gate, no accuracy risk. That is the single best
   payoff-to-risk item in the plan.
2. The **solve-only** speedup of the full stack is ~11×, better than the
   "7–10×" previously claimed. But it requires *both* perturbing phases.
3. **End-to-end the full stack is 3.9×, and parse becomes 84 % of the runtime.**
   After Phase 3 this is a **parse-bound program**. Phase 1 as previously scoped
   recovers ~26 s of the 44 s; the remaining ~18 s is unexplained and needs its
   own profile. Without that work every further GPU optimisation is
   Amdahl-capped at ~1.2× additional end-to-end.
4. **The GPU is not the biggest single item — the stamping loop is.** After
   Phase 3a the array spends 11.6 s stamping and 0.11 s on the GPU. Phase 3b is
   what converts a 2.6× into a 3.9×, and it is a *CPU* change.

**Routing consequences.** Parse is promoted from a "free win" footnote to a
first-class phase (§6, Phase 1). And the two perturbing phases (3a GPU, 3b
stamping) should be **gated together**, since neither alone reaches the target.

### 2.4t The transient, **measured** — and the cost class every revision missed **[M — NEW, rev 4]**

Rev 3's §2.4 (kept below) *extrapolated* a transient budget from `.op`
per-iteration costs and an assumed 4 NR iterations/step. Rev 4 measured a
real SRAM **write transient** — wl0 pulsed, bl0 driven low, column 0 flips,
every other cell holds — through the production two-stage path
(`run_transient`'s knobs: DC OP from `.ic`, then `TransientSolver` seeded
from the OP). 200 steps of 50 ps over 10 ns.
**[M — `scripts/bench_gpu/prof_tran.py`, box loadavg ~1390/192 (contended):
wall times are indicative, call counts and shares are exact.]**

Instrumented counters, no profiler (`.tran` = 200 steps, 483 NR iterations;
OP = 41 NR iterations at both sizes):

| array | devices | stage | wall | nn (batched) | stamp (incl. transcap) | spsolve | commit + rest | rest share |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4×4 | 96 | OP | 0.69 s | 0.48 | 0.15 | 0.01 | 0.05 | — |
| 4×4 | 96 | **`.tran`** | **85.6 s** | 8.4 | 5.4 (dc 2.6) | 0.31 | **71.4** | **83 %** |
| 8×8 | 384 | OP | 2.23 s | 1.49 | 0.59 | 0.03 | 0.12 | — |
| 8×8 | 384 | **`.tran`** | **204.8 s** | 19.1 | 15.8 (dc 7.4) | 0.90 | **168.9** | **82 %** |

Three measured facts:

1. **NR iterations per accepted step = 2.42 at both sizes** (483 batch evals
   / 200 steps — identical count at 4×4 and 8×8), not the 4 rev 3 assumed.
   **The LM ladder fires in transient**: 620/621 solves vs 483 batch evals =
   +28 % extra factorisations at both sizes — rev 3's §2 caveat ("report both
   counters") was warranted. No dt-halving occurred. **[M]**
2. **The transcap stamp roughly doubles the stamping loop** — 5.4 s total vs
   2.6 s for the DC part at 4×4. Phase 3b's batched stamping must cover the
   `_stamp_mosfet_transient` charge/capacitance block (`solver.py:1826-1930`),
   not just `_stamp_mosfet_dc`, or it recovers half the cost. **[M]**
3. **The dominant term is none of the above.** 71.4 s — **83 % of the
   transient** — sits outside every bucket the `.op` model knew about.
   cProfile attributes it: `get_charges` — 84,864 calls, **70.4 s
   cumulative** — via **19,296 cache-cold single-row `_eval`s** (1 forward +
   3 backwards each, 3.65 ms/call). **[M]**

**The commit-path pathology, precisely.** After each accepted timestep the
solver commits charge history per device: `get_charges(timestep_voltages)` at
`solver.py:2238`, then `update_charge_state()` (which calls `get_charges`
again — that one hits the per-device `_eval` cache). `timestep_voltages` is
the *solved* solution of the step — a voltage set the NR-loop
`_batch_eval_nn_mosfets` never evaluated, so the warm cache never matches.
The arithmetic is exact and beautiful in its badness: 200 steps × 96 devices
+ 96 init = **19,296 forwards**; 3 backwards each = 57,888 of the 59,586
measured `run_backward` calls. Every NR-loop `_eval` hits the warm batch
cache; every commit-path `_eval` misses. **[M]**

Scaling is linear in N by construction — the commit path is N independent
batch-1 evals whose per-call cost does not fall with N (weight-streaming-bound
per V7.0.0). Measured per-call cost varies 2.2–3.7 ms across runs on the
contended box (4×4: 3.7 ms; 8×8: 2.2 ms); projections below use the
conservative 3.65 ms and carry that ±40 % until Phase 0's quiet-box re-run:

| array | devices | commit cost / step | × 200 steps |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4×4 | 96 | 0.35 s *(measured)* | 70 s *(measured)* |
| 8×8 | 384 | 0.84 s *(measured)* | 169 s *(measured)* |
| 32×32 | 6,144 | **13–22 s** | 45–75 min |
| 256×128 | 196,608 | **~7–12 min** | **~24–40 h** |

**The fix is the same class as §5.1's AC fix: one
`_batch_eval_nn_mosfets(circuit, timestep_voltages)` before the commit loop**
warms every cache in a single batched call (0.61 s CPU at 6144, 2.6 ms GPU).
~10 lines. It is **perturbing** — batched GEMM rows and a single-row eval
differ in float32 (the V7.0.0 `F.linear` row-stability result), and the
committed charge history feeds every subsequent step — so it ships
**default-off** behind `PYCIRCUITSIM_TRAN_BATCH_COMMIT=1` and gates with the
other perturbing work (§8). It is nonetheless the highest-payoff item in this
plan: nothing else touches 75–83 % of the transient.

> **Why no gate ever caught this:** the complex-circuit transients gate
> *accuracy*, not cost, and at ≤ 20 devices the commit path is milliseconds.
> It is a pure scaling pathology — invisible at gate size, dominant at array
> size. (`.ac` had the same disease in §5.1, also found only by reading the
> code path at scale.)

### 3.1 The shape is already right

`batch_eval` groups devices by shared `nn.Module` identity, and an SRAM array
resolves to exactly **two** checkpoints no matter how big it gets. **[M —
verified in code: the group key is `id(m._nn_model)` (`mosfet_nn.py:556`) and

Splitting *does* occur on **TECH** and **LEVEL**, and on a per-instance

### 3.3 The blocker: per-device host readback — measured, not predicted

`batch_eval` computes on the GPU, then hands `out[i]`, `grad_id[i]` to
`_unpack_eval` **one device at a time**, and `_unpack_eval` calls `.tolist()` on
those slices (`mosfet_nn.py:403-404`, `:444-445` — up to four per device). On
CUDA that is **one device→host synchronisation per MOSFET per NR iteration**.

Running the array through the latent GPU path today (`CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=1`,
everything else unchanged) confirms the consequence. **[M —
`prof_split.py`; note this is the §3.3 configuration, not §2's]**

| array | solve CPU | solve **GPU** | tensor CPU→GPU | tail CPU→GPU | µs/unpack CPU→GPU |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 8×8 (384 dev) | 2.65 s | **5.82 s** (2.2× *slower*) | 1.49 → 2.43 s | 0.40 → 1.73 s | 25.4 → **110.0** |
| 16×16 (1536 dev) | 11.21 s | **12.11 s** (1.08× slower) | 5.50 → **2.27 s** | 2.02 → **5.32 s** | 32.1 → **84.5** |

Read the 16×16 row carefully — it is the whole argument in one line. The GPU
**did** do its job on the tensor work: 5.50 s → 2.27 s. But the Python tail
simultaneously went 2.02 s → 5.32 s, because every one of the 62,976
`_unpack_eval` calls now blocks on a device sync. The two cancel. **Net gain
from switching on the GPU today: zero, and negative on smaller arrays.**

### 3.3b The mirror-image blocker on the *input* side

`batch_eval` also assembles its input per device, every NR iteration
(`mosfet_nn.py:565-572`):

```python
v_raw = torch.tensor(raw_v[key], ..., device=ref._device)  # H2D from a Python list
x_g   = torch.stack([m._geo_norm_t for m in devs], dim=0)  # N separate device tensors
tech  = torch.cat([m._tech_code_tensor for m in devs], dim=0)
```

`_geo_norm_t` and `_tech_code_tensor` are **per-device tensors already resident
on the GPU**, so the stack/cat are N tiny device-to-device kernel launches.
Measured against the batched equivalent: **23× at N=384, 185× at 1536, 384× at
6144.** **[M]**

> **Correctness hazard the previous draft missed.** `batch_eval` *skips devices
> whose cache is already warm* (`mosfet_nn.py:551-559`), so **group membership
> varies between NR iterations**. In a large settled array many cells hold their
> terminal voltages between iterations, so this path is exercised, not
> hypothetical. A Phase-2c cache keyed on "the group" would silently mis-pair
> geometry rows with devices. **It must be keyed on the actual member list — or
> better, hold one full-array tensor and index it with a per-iteration row-index
> array, which sidesteps invalidation entirely.** **[M]**

### 3.3c What this means for the port

Four changes, not one:

- **cache the stacked geometry/tech-code** per group (§3.3b) — immutable data,
  currently re-stacked every iteration, with the membership caveat above;
- build the voltage batch on-device in one op from a **contiguous numpy array**,
  not from a per-device Python list;
- run forward + autograd batched (already done);
- **one** D2H transfer of the whole `(N, 13)` result + Jacobians, then vectorise
  the denorm/guard/Vds-correction tail over the batch (§3.3).

The last is Phase 2a, and it is the precondition — not an optimisation — for
the GPU being worth anything at all.

### 3.4 Data movement — the bus is never the problem, the marshalling is

**The NR loop is CPU-resident.** MNA assembly, `spsolve` and the convergence
tests all run on the host, so every NR iteration is a **synchronous round trip**:
push voltages, wait, pull back currents and Jacobians. The solver cannot step
until the results land, so *none of this can be hidden behind async compute*.
Per iteration, per group:

| direction | payload | bytes | at N=6144 |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| H2D | voltages `(N,4)` float32 | 16 N | 96 KB |
| D2H | `out (N,13)` + `grad_id (N,4)` float32 | 68 N | 408 KB |
| D2H | + `grad_qg`, `grad_qd` (transient) | 100 N | 600 KB |

These are *tiny* — 500 KB moves in tens of microseconds. §3.2's end-to-end table
is the authoritative cost; the component breakdown below explains *why* the
"today" column is 7.7× worse.

- **H2D**: `torch.tensor(list_of_N_tuples, device=cuda)` must traverse 4N Python
  floats before any bytes reach the bus.
- **D2H**: `.tolist()` rebuilds N×13 Python floats; `.cpu()` into numpy is
  orders cheaper. And today it is not even batched — it runs *per device*.

**The irreducible part: round-trip latency.** Measured on an **idle** 4090
(`scripts/bench_gpu/lat.py`): **[M]**

| operation | median |
| --- | ---: |
| empty `synchronize()` | 8.0 µs |
| tiny kernel + sync | 15.2 µs |
| H2D 96 KB pinned + sync | 17.5 µs |
| D2H 408 KB → numpy + sync | 61.5 µs |
| **full round trip at N=6144** | **56.8 µs** |

> ~~The previous draft quoted 98–137 µs from contended devices and guessed
> "~20–40 µs idle". The true idle floor is **56.8 µs** — about half the
> contended figure, not a quarter.~~ **[M]**

That floor is paid *per NR iteration* for as long as the solver stays on the
host, and no buffer tuning removes it. Recomputed at 56.8 µs:

| workload | round trips | latency cost | vs compute |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| `.op`, 6144 devices | 41 × 2 groups = 82 | **4.7 ms** | negligible vs 3.5 s solve |
| `.tran`, 6144 dev, 10⁴ steps × 4 NR | 80,000 | **4.5 s** | GPU NR cost is 40,000 × 2.58 ms = 103 s → **4.4 %**. Tolerable. **[E]** |
| **one 6-device cell, any analysis** | per iteration | ~57 µs | compute is ~10 µs — **latency is ~6× the work** |

That last row is the important one. **The latency floor makes the GPU useless
for small circuits, no matter how well the transfers are written.** The only way
to amortise it is to batch across *circuits* — Track B (§9). Data movement is
therefore an independent argument for Phase 5.

> ~~The previous draft stated the transient latency share was "~1–2 %". At the
> corrected floor and the corrected per-iteration GPU cost it is **4.4 %**.~~ **[E]**

**Multi-GPU.** `nvidia-smi topo -m` reports **NODE** for all three pairs —
PCIe host bridge, **no NVLink**. **[M]** Splitting *one* array across 3 GPUs
would need an inter-GPU exchange inside every NR iteration, adding round trips
to a path already latency-bound, for a compute term that is not the bottleneck.
At N=6144 the entire GPU round trip is 1.57 ms of which 56.8 µs is latency;
a 3-way split would cut ~1.5 ms of compute while adding at least two more
synchronisations. **Do not do it for Track A.** Track B is the opposite case:
Monte Carlo samples are independent, so they partition across GPUs with **zero**
inter-GPU communication and scale ~3× for free.

### 3.6 Options the previous draft never considered **[NEW]**

Four designs beyond "set the device", each judged on payoff / accuracy risk /
effort. One of them changes the ceiling materially.

**(a) CUDA Graphs — defer for `.op`, do it for `.tran`.** Shapes are static
across NR iterations, so capture-and-replay applies (including the backward, via
`make_graphed_callables`). It removes essentially all of the ~0.95 ms fixed cost:
at 6144, 1.57 → ~0.68 ms. **But in situ for a 32×32 `.op` that is 0.11 s → 0.05 s
of a 14 s solve — 0.3 %, not worth it.** For a 10⁴-step `.tran` it is ~48 s of a
128 s GPU total (1.6×) — worth it, *after* the host Python is gone (Phases
2 + 2t + 3b), since before then the GPU is a few % of the run. Rev 4 raises
its standing: the transient is now the target, and the batched commit adds
200 more identically-shaped calls per op. Replay executes identical kernels, so it
is bit-identical to the un-graphed GPU path: **zero incremental accuracy risk**.
Effort high (static buffers, no host control flow or `.item()` inside capture).
*Verdict: Phase 3d, `.tran`-only, after Phases 2/2t.*

**(b) On-device gather of node voltages — fold into Phase 2c.** Today the H2D
input is built from a Python list of N tuples: at 6144 devices that is 24,576
dict `.get()` calls per NR iteration. Rather than full device residency (the host
still needs `voltages` for stamping and the convergence test), precompute a
per-group `(N,4) int64` index into the solution vector, push the whole float64
solution (2145 nodes × 8 B = **17 KB**, ~10 µs) and gather on-device. 17 KB up
instead of 96 KB, and **zero Python per device**. The source shift `v_d − v_s`
must be done in float64 on the gathered vector *before* the cast to stay exact.
*Verdict: fold into Phase 2c — low effort, self-contained, and it is what makes
(c) possible.*

**(c) MNA assembly on the GPU via a precomputed scatter map — the biggest idea
missing from the previous draft.** Precompute once, for every stamp
contribution, its flat position in a preallocated CSR `data` array; assembly
becomes one `segment_reduce`. The prize is not the assembly kernel — it is that
**the whole chain from NN output to CSR values becomes device-resident**,
deleting *both* the tail and the stamping loop from the host:

```
[device] forward+autograd → denorm → guard_gds → Vds correction
       → g_ds/g_m/g_mb/i_eq → scatter into preallocated CSR data + rhs
[D2H]  csr_data (16,772 × 8 B = 134 KB) + rhs (17 KB)   ← the ONLY transfer
[host] spsolve
```

Post-(c) budget at 32×32: 0.11 (NN) + ~0.05 (on-device denorm+scatter) + 1.9
(`other`) + 0.25 (solve) ≈ **2.3 s solve**, versus 3.6 s for the plan's best
case. **[E]**

> **Accuracy trap — record this now.** `index_add_` / `scatter_add_` on CUDA use
> atomics and are therefore **non-deterministic run-to-run on the same GPU**.
> For a bistable 6T latch that means the identical netlist can land different NR
> basins on two consecutive runs of the same binary — **strictly worse than
> anything in this repo's scar tissue, because it is not reproducible.**
> Mitigations in order: (i) sort the scatter map once by destination and use
> `torch.segment_reduce`; (ii) `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` +
> `CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG=:4096:8`, which routes `index_add_` to a
> deterministic sorted implementation at a throughput cost irrelevant here.
> **Non-negotiable: a 100-run identical-output test before any gate runs on this
> path.**

*Verdict: Phase 3c, explicitly out of scope for the first GPU landing — but
recorded now, because designing Phase 3a's D2H boundary without knowing about it
produces an interface that has to be rewritten.*

**(d) GPU sparse LU / cuDSS — still no, but §4's reasoning needs a fix.** cuDSS
does have the analysis/factorise/solve split with a refactorisation entry point
(the GPU analogue of KLU). §4's *structural* objection (tiny supernodes) is
correct and decisive. §4's *Amdahl* objection is **not durable**: 0.55 % is a
ratio against today's constant factors, and in the post-(c) budget the solve is
0.25 s of 2.3 s = **11 %**, the second-largest term. *Verdict: keep the "no",
restate the reason as "not the **first** target".*

## 4. Option B — ship the solver to the GPU

**Recommendation: no.** Three independent reasons.

1. **Amdahl says it is not the *first* target.** 0.55 % of the solve today; a
   solver that took *zero* time would make the 32×32 solve 1.005× faster.

   > **Methodological rule this plan must follow: re-derive the Amdahl share at
   > every phase boundary, not once.** 0.55 % is a ratio against *today's*
   > constant factors. After Phase 3a the solve is 0.25 s of 14.0 s = **1.8 %**;
   > in the §3.6(c) budget it is 0.25 s of 2.3 s = **11 %**, the second-largest
   > term. A plan whose entire argument is Amdahl cannot compute the ratio once
   > and treat it as durable — every phase changes the denominator.
2. **Circuit matrices are the worst case for GPU sparse LU.** MNA matrices are
   extremely sparse (~4–8 nnz/row), asymmetric and irregular. GPU sparse direct
   solvers win by finding dense supernodes to feed dense kernels; circuit
   matrices have tiny supernodes, so the GPU runs at a fraction of peak while
   paying transfer and launch latency. This is why production SPICE uses KLU (a
   CPU code designed for circuit sparsity), not a GPU solver.
3. **The cheap CPU win is unclaimed.** `_solve_mna` calls `spsolve` fresh every
   NR iteration — full symbolic analysis + numeric factorisation + solve, when
   the *topology* never changes. Any argument for GPU solving has to beat a
   refactorising CPU baseline, not the current one.

   > **Correction — the sparsity pattern is NOT iteration-invariant today, and
   > this plan previously asserted twice that it was. [M]** `_stamp_mosfet_dc`
   > gates four matrix entries (`[d,b]`, `[d,s]`, `[s,b]`, `[s,s]`) on
   > `abs(g_mb) > 1e-12` (`solver.py:366`). And `gmb` is set to **exactly 0.0**
   > by the wrong-sign clamp in `_apply_vds_correction`
   > (`mosfet_nn.py:863-866`), and multiplied by a factor `f_id` that goes to 0
   > as `|Vds| → 0` (`:848`). **So every device passing through Vds≈0 or the
   > reverse-conduction region drops those four entries mid-NR** — in a
   > switching SRAM array, a large and iteration-varying fraction of devices.
   >
   > Consequence: a KLU refactorise-with-existing-pivots design that assumes a
   > static pattern would **silently drop those entries**. The fix is trivial —
   > always emit the four `g_mb` triplets, value 0 below threshold — but it is a
   > *behaviour change to the assembled matrix* (explicit zeros change nothing
   > numerically, but `spsolve`'s pivoting sees a different matrix), so it must
   > be stated, implemented deliberately, and gated. This applies equally to
   > Phase 3b's COO builder.

The one scenario that changes this verdict is arrays large enough that LU
fill-in on the bitline/wordline nodes dominates — §5. That is a real crossover,
but far outside the sizes where the NN cost is the problem.

## 5. Where the solver *does* eventually become the bottleneck

The bitline and `vdd` nodes are the risk: every cell in a column touches `bl_c`,
and every cell touches `vdd`, so those nodes have degree ~2·rows and ~2·cells.
High-degree nodes drive LU fill-in superlinearly. Measured on a **synthetic**
matrix with the array's connectivity (values randomised, diagonally dominant),
single-threaded **[M — `bench_spsolve.py`]**:

| array | cells | N | nnz | `spsolve` | `splu` factor+solve | `splu` **solve only** | LU nnz | fill |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 8×8 | 64 | 178 | 1,124 | 0.66 ms | 0.65 ms | **0.027 ms** | 15,446 | 13.7× |
| 16×16 | 256 | 610 | 4,292 | 2.87 ms | 2.87 ms | **0.082 ms** | 61,446 | 14.3× |
| 32×32 | 1,024 | 2,242 | 16,772 | 67 ms | 67 ms | **2.2 ms** | 773,476 | 46.1× |
| 64×64 | 4,096 | 8,578 | 66,308 | 3.03 s | 6.78 s | **16.7 ms** | 10,858,454 | 163.8× |
| 96×96 | 9,216 | 19,010 | 148,612 | 34.9 s | 21.9 s | **56.0 ms** | 31,132,392 | 209.5× |

> **Read this table with care — it overstates the problem.** At 32×32 the
> synthetic takes 67 ms per solve, but the *real* MNA at the same size measured
> 5.4 ms (0.22 s / 41 solves) — the synthetic is **~12× pessimistic**, because
> randomised values defeat pivoting differently than real device conductances
> do. Treat the absolute times as an upper bound and the *shape* as the finding.
> A faithful large-array measurement needs the real assembled matrix, which
> Phase 1 (fast parse) makes cheap to obtain; **do that before acting on this
> section.** **[A]**

Two things survive the caveat:

1. **There is a real crossover.** Fill-in grows 13.7× → 209.5×, and the solve
   grows far faster than `O(N)`. Dividing the synthetic by 12, the 64×64 solve
   is ~250 ms × 41 = ~10 s — against a Phase-3 NN tensor cost of ~0.4 s at that
   size. **So the crossover lands near 64×64, not "past ~4k cells" as previously
   written — inside the stretch target of §0, not outside it.** **[E]**
2. **The headroom is refactorisation, not the GPU.** `splu`'s *solve-only* step
   is **30–600× cheaper** than a full `spsolve`. The MNA *topology* is fixed for
   the circuit's lifetime — but see the correction in §4.3: the assembled
   pattern is not invariant today, and making it so is a prerequisite for any
   refactorising solver.

**Correction to a tempting shortcut:** "just use `splu` instead of `spsolve`"
does *not* capture this. The table shows `splu` factor+solve measuring the same
as `spsolve` (67.4 vs 67.2 ms at 32×32) — the numeric factorisation, not the
symbolic analysis, is the expensive part, and scipy exposes no
refactorise-with-existing-pivots API. Capturing the 30–600× requires a solver
with a refactorisation entry point — **KLU**. That is a CPU library, and it
*reinforces* §4: the answer to a slow circuit solve is KLU, not CUDA.

### 5.1 `.ac` is a different regime, and none of the above applies to it **[M — NEW]**

Everything in §2–§5 is measured on `.op` and extends to `.tran`. **It is flatly
wrong for `.ac`**, and the previous draft never checked:

* **`ACSolver` never calls `_batch_eval_nn_mosfets`.** The four call sites are
  all DC/transient (`solver.py:760, :909, :1100, :1578`). Instead
  `_precompute_mosfet_small_signal` (`solver.py:2621-2649`) loops **per device**,
  calling `get_conductance` and `get_capacitances` — N *unbatched* batch-1
  forward+3-backward evals.
* **Worse, they are guaranteed cold.** `ACSolver.__init__` calls
  `_require_nn_caps` (`solver.py:2449`), which `clear_cache()`s every device
  whose cache lacks caps. So the precompute pass always runs cold: at 6144
  devices that is 6144 batch-1 evals ≈ **5 s of pure launch-bound work**, and
  **Phase 3 makes it worse, not better** (batch-1 is the one regime where the
  GPU loses — §3.2). Calling `batch_eval` once before the precompute loop is a
  ~3-line fix this plan should name.
* **The AC matrix is DENSE.** `solver.py:2506` allocates
  `np.zeros((matrix_size, matrix_size), dtype=complex)` and solves it with
  `np.linalg.solve` (`:2526`) — **per frequency**. At a 32×32 array's 2145 nodes
  that is 2145² × 16 B = **73.6 MB per frequency point** and an O(N³) dense LU;
  a 100-point sweep is ~7.4 GB of allocation churn. **§4's "MNA matrices are
  extremely sparse" and §2's "the solve is 0.55 %" do not hold for `.ac`, where
  the solver is the entire cost and the NN is a one-off.**

**Routing consequence.** `.ac` on large arrays is its own problem with its own
answer (batch the precompute; make the AC matrix sparse). Phase 0 re-profiles
`.tran`; **it must re-profile `.ac` too**, and this plan's conclusions must be
labelled `.op`/`.tran`-only until it does.

### 5.2 The fill-in explosion is an ORDERING ARTEFACT — one parameter fixes it **[M — NEW, rev 3]**

The fill-in growth above (13.7× → 399×) is **not inherent to circuit matrices**.
It is scipy's default `COLAMD` column ordering, which `spsolve` uses. MNA
matrices are **structurally symmetric**, for which `MMD_AT_PLUS_A` is the
textbook choice. Measured on the same synthetic matrices:

| array | N | ordering | factor time | fill | speedup |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 64×32 | 4,354 | COLAMD *(today)* | 472.7 ms | 86.0× | — |
| | | **MMD_AT_PLUS_A** | **24.6 ms** | **1.6×** | **19×** |
| 128×64 | 16,898 | COLAMD *(today)* | 29,296 ms | 315.4× | — |
| | | **MMD_AT_PLUS_A** | **525.7 ms** | **1.6×** | **56×** |
| 192×96 | 37,634 | COLAMD *(today)* | 124,502 ms | 399.0× | — |
| | | **MMD_AT_PLUS_A** | **1,783 ms** | **1.6×** | **70×** |

**Fill stays flat at 1.6× across every size**, against COLAMD's runaway growth.
That converts the solver at 256×128 from ~1,370 s to **14.4 s** — the difference
between "the solver is 1000× the GPU NN" and "the solver is 10× the GPU NN".

> **Robustness check — and a trap I nearly fell into. [M]** `NATURAL` ordering
> looked even better (112 ms, 2.5× fill at 192×96) — but that is an artefact of
> the synthetic builder emitting nodes in cell order. Real circuits go through
> `Circuit.get_nodes()`, which returns `sorted(...)` — alphabetical. Re-testing
> at 128×64 under three node orderings:
>
> | ordering of the nodes | COLAMD | **MMD_AT_PLUS_A** | NATURAL |
> | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
> | builder-natural | 315× fill | **1.6×** | 2.5× |
> | **alphabetical (what the parser produces)** | 315× | **1.6×** | **444×** |
> | random permutation | 315× | **1.6×** | **849×** |
>
> **`MMD_AT_PLUS_A` is completely invariant to node ordering; `NATURAL` collapses
> under the ordering the code actually produces.** Do not ship `NATURAL`.

**Implementation.** `_solve_mna` currently calls `spsolve(A.tocsr(), rhs)`
(`solver.py:62`). The change is `splu(A.tocsc(), permc_spec="MMD_AT_PLUS_A").solve(rhs)`.
This is **perturbing** — a different pivot order is different floating-point
arithmetic — so it ships default-off behind a flag and is gated with the other
perturbing work. It is the highest payoff-per-line change on the *solver*.
~~It also makes the KLU investigation (§7 Phase 4b) much less urgent~~ —
**rev 4 reverses half of that**: on `.op` (41 factorisations) ordering alone
suffices, but a 200-step transient factorises ~620 times and a 10⁴-step run
~3×10⁴ times, all on an unchanged sparsity pattern (once §4.3's `g_mb`
always-emit fix lands). The `splu` **solve-only** step is 30–600× cheaper
than a fresh factorisation, so a refactorise-with-existing-pivots design
(KLU, or scipy `splu` reuse with numeric-only refresh) is **re-promoted for
transient work at ≥64×32** — after 4a′ is measured on real matrices. §7
Phase 4b.

> **Caveat, stated plainly.** These are synthetic matrices with randomised values
> (§5's ~12× pessimism caveat applies). Fill-in is a function of *structure*, so
> the 1.6×-vs-399× contrast is solid; the absolute times are not. **Phase 1 must
> re-measure on the real assembled matrix before the ordering change is
> promoted** — a real MNA's pivoting may reorder for numerical reasons. **[A]**

## 6. Parse — first-class for `.op` and MC; amortised on the transient

Parse is 44.4 s at 6144 devices, more than the `.op` solve, and §2.3 shows it
becomes **84 % of an `.op` run** once Phase 3 lands. It is not a scaling law.
**Rev 4 demotes it for the transient workload**: over 483 NR iterations + 200
commits it is 1–8 % today, returning to ~30 % only at the end of the fix
ladder (§2.6t). The work below stays — it is bit-identical, it gates the MC
parse-once design (§9), and it decides the end-state — but it no longer runs
first.

**Known cause #1 — one `torch.load` per device.** `_MOSFETNNBase.__init__`
(`mosfet_nn.py:193`) calls

```python
state = torch.load(str(model_path_obj), weights_only=True, map_location="cpu")
```

**unconditionally**, and only *afterwards* consults `_SHARED_NN_MODULES`
(`:205`) to decide whether to build a module from it. On a cache hit the freshly
deserialised `state` is discarded. **[M]** Measured by counting calls during an
8×8 parse:

```
parse=2.04s  nn_devices=384  torch.load calls=384 totalling 1.12s
```

**55 % of parse time**, re-deserialising the same 3.6 MB file 384 times to throw
383 away. At 6144 devices that is ~22 GB of pointless I/O and unpickling,
~18 s of the 44 s.

**Known cause #2 — `NormStats.load` per device** (`mosfet_nn.py:215`), ~1.3 ms
each ≈ **8 s** at 6144 devices. **[M]**

**Known cause #3 — extra per-device file reads on LEVEL=74/75** that the
previous draft did not count: `np.load` of the `*_config.npz` sidecar
(`mosfet_bsimar.py:56`, `mosfet_pfn.py:57`), executed even on a cache hit; and
for L74 a `BSIMARNormStats.load` *inside the resolver*, per device
(`parser.py:264-266`). Phase 1 must cover these or the fix is DirectNet-only. **[M]**

**Known cause #4 — per-device stdout.** `_resolve_nn_checkpoint` prints one
`[NN-resolver]` line per MOSFET instance (`parser.py:324-325`) — 6144 lines for
a 32×32 array — plus a per-device WARNING print at `:347` on unknown tech codes
and a per-model-card FORCE-LEVEL print at `:967-968`. **[M]**

**Unexplained residue: ~18 s.** Causes 1–3 account for ~26 s of the 44 s.
**Phase 1 must profile the remainder before it is declared done** — at 84 % of
post-Phase-3 runtime, an unexplained 18 s is the largest single unknown in this
plan.

Fix for #1–#3: consult the cache key first, and only load on a miss. Strictly
bit-identical (the weights are the same object either way), taking parse from
`O(devices × checkpoint)` to `O(devices + checkpoints)`.

> Note for whoever implements this: an isolated microbenchmark of `torch.load`
> on this file reports 15 ms/call, which would predict 93 s at 6144 devices —
> more than the entire measured parse. The in-situ cost is 2.9 ms. **Trust the
> call-counting measurement, not the microbenchmark**; the isolated loop does
> not reproduce the allocator and page-cache state of a real parse.

## 7. Routing — what to build, in order

Two tracks over a shared base; **both** are in scope (§9).

```
Phase 0   transient + .ac profiles (4x4 tran DONE, §2.4t) + branch proximity ─┐
Phase 1   parse: cache-first load (1a), CPU-default device (1b),              ├─ shared
          profile the unexplained residue (1c)                                │   BIT-IDENTICAL
Phase 2   2a-lite  single D2H -> numpy (~10 lines)                            │   default-on
          2c       cached stacked group inputs                                │
          2a-full  vectorised denorm tail · 2d vectorise `other`             ─┘
                    │
         ┌──────────┴──────────┐
   Track B                 Track A                       PERTURBING, default-off
   Phase 5a cross-circuit  Phase 2t batch the commit-path eval (§2.4t)
            batch_eval     Phase 3a GPU NN eval          gated together (§8.4)
   Phase 5b batched        Phase 3b batched stamping (incl. transcap)
            on-device NR   Phase 4a solver hygiene (bit-identical)
                           Phase 4a' MMD ordering · 4b refactorisation
```

**Ordering is by measured payoff per unit of risk; the phase numbers do not
imply execution order.** Explicitly: **0 → 1 → 2 → {2t+3a+3b together}**, with
Track B's parse-once (9a) schedulable immediately.

> ### Priority order on the transient workload (rev 4)
>
> The phase *numbering* is inherited from revision 1 and no longer matches
> payoff. Ranked by impact on a 200-step write op (§2.4t, §2.6t):
>
> | rank | work | payoff | risk | phase |
> | ---: | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | **1** | **batch the commit-path eval** | **60–85 % of the transient at every size** (measured 75 % at 4×4) | perturbing (single-row → batched), ~10 lines | **2t** |
> | **2** | GPU NN eval (carries NR loop *and* batched commit) | 611 ms → 2.6 ms per transient iteration at 6144 | perturbing | 3a |
> | **3** | vectorise tail + stamping (incl. transcap) + `other` | ~6.6× on the NR loop, ×483 iterations/op | mixed | 2 / 3b |
> | **4** | **solver: MMD ordering, then refactorisation** | 76× per factorisation; ~620 factorisations/op, 3×10⁴ per 10⁴-step run | perturbing | 4a′ / 4b |
> | **5** | parse `O(devices)` → `O(devices + checkpoints)` | 1–8 % of a transient today; ~30 % at end-state; gates MC parse-once | bit-identical | 1a/1c |
> | **6** | parse-once for MC | removes the per-sample parse entirely | plumbing + state-reset hazard | 9a |
>
> ~~Rev 3's ranking (parse 83 % first, GPU fifth)~~ was correct for `.op` and
> is kept in §2.6 for that workload. On the transient the GPU jumps to
> second: it is the only thing that touches both NN terms at once.

> **Correction to the previous draft's dependency claim.** It stated Phase 2a is
> "the precondition — not an optimisation — for the GPU being worth anything at
> all", and made Track A depend on a phase it simultaneously admitted might fail
> its own bit-exactness criterion. That is a schedule risk with no technical
> basis. The measured zero-gain of §3.3 is not a property of "GPU without
> vectorised Python" — it is a property of **one line**: `_unpack_eval` calling
> `.tolist()` on a *CUDA* tensor slice, once per device. Moving the D2H boundary
> out of the per-device loop fixes it in ~10 lines:
>
> ```python
> out_np = out.detach().cpu().numpy()        # one D2H, (N,13)
> gid_np = grad_id.detach().cpu().numpy()    # one D2H, (N,4)
> m._eval_cache = m._unpack_eval(out_np[i], gid_np[i], ...)   # loop unchanged
> ```
>
> `_unpack_eval` already begins with `out_row.tolist()`, and `ndarray.tolist()`
> on a float32 array yields the identical Python floats as `Tensor.tolist()` on
> a float32 tensor. **This is bit-identical by inspection, carries no
> summation-order proof obligation, and unblocks the GPU on its own.** It is
> `2a-lite`, and Phase 3 depends on it rather than on the full vectorisation.

Phases 0–2 and 4a are bit-identical and ship default-on. Phases 3a, 3b, 4b and
5 perturb float ordering and ship default-off behind flags.

## 8. Validation regime

Perturbation here is not the smooth "~1e-8 in the NN output" the previous draft
assumed. Two hard discontinuities sit exactly where an SRAM latch operates:

* **`_guard_gds`** (`mosfet_nn.py:738-740`): `if gds_phys > 0.0: return gds_phys;
  return max(|id|·0.02, 1e-12)`. A 1-ULP sign change at the boundary jumps the
  output from ~0⁺ to `|id|/50 V` — at `id = 10 µA` a step of **2e-7 S** against
  `GMIN = 1e-12 S`. Five orders, from a last-bit change.
* **The wrong-sign clamp** (`mosfet_nn.py:855-866`) sets `id`, `gm`, `gmb` to
  **exactly 0.0**. Perturbation across `id = 0` is a full-magnitude
  discontinuity.

Both are hit at `id ≈ 0`, `Vds ≈ 0` — the SRAM off-device regime. This is the
concrete path from a float32 GEMM reordering to an O(1) stamp change, and the
direct descendant of the gds sign+floor bug (CLAUDE.md Rule 4). Phase 0
instruments it.

### 8.1 Two hard constraints that make Phase 2a bit-identical **[M]**

**Constraint 1 — the exponential must stay `math.exp`.**
`_apply_vds_correction` uses `math.exp` (libm). A vectorised rewrite naturally
reaches for `np.exp`; that substitution is **not** value-preserving:

| pair | mismatching elements |
| --- | --- |
| `np.exp(array)` vs `math.exp` | **9,232 / 200,000 (4.6 %), 1 ULP** |
| `np.sinh(array)` vs `np.sinh(scalar)` | 0 / 200,000 |
| `np.sqrt` vs `math.sqrt` | 0 / 100,000 |
| `np.sinh` vs `math.sinh` | 52,131 / 200,000 (26 %) — **`math.sinh` is prohibited** |

End-to-end on the real production checkpoint at N=6144
(`scripts/bench_gpu/bench_tail.py`):

| batched-tail variant | `id` bit-exact | max rel err | cost |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `np.exp` | 98.54 % | 1.7e-12 | 0.95 ms |
| **masked per-element `math.exp`** | **100.00 %** | **0** | 1.33 ms |

> The exponential MUST be evaluated as `math.exp` per element over a
> **boolean-masked subset** — the elements that actually reach the exp branch,
> i.e. not short-circuited by the `normal_dir and abs_vds > 20·VT` fast path.
> `np.exp`, `torch.exp` and any SIMD exponential are **prohibited** on this
> path. The remaining ~10 operations vectorise normally; only the transcendental
> is looped. The constraint costs 0.38 ms per NR iteration at N=6144 and still
> leaves the tail 58× faster than today.

Why the single ULP matters more than its size: `f_sym = 1.0 - exp_sym` is a
cancellation. At `|Vds| = 1 mV` with `VT ≈ 0.06 V`, `f_sym ≈ 0.0165` — a **60×
amplification** — and `|Vds| ≈ 0` is precisely the SRAM off-device and
pass-transistor regime.

**Constraint 2 — cast to float64 before any arithmetic.** `.tolist()` yields
Python floats, so the tail currently runs in float64. `.cpu().numpy()` yields
**float32**, and under NumPy 2 (NEP-50) `float32_array * python_float` **stays
float32** — verified on numpy 2.2.6. The naive rewrite would silently run the
whole denorm chain in float32: measured relative error **median 3.6e-8, max
2.2e-7**, i.e. **worse than `VNTOL = 1e-7` itself**, and ~10⁹× larger than the
1e-16 the phase is worrying about. It produces plausible output and passes smoke
tests. Mandatory: `.cpu().numpy().astype(np.float64)` before any arithmetic,
plus `assert arr.dtype == np.float64` at entry and exit of the batched tail. **[M]**

### 8.2 The suite has no gated bistable test — and that is the risk

`tests/verify_complex_sram_snm.py` gates the **half-cell open-loop butterfly
lobe**; the full cross-coupled 6T `force_ic` solve is a printed **diagnostic
that does not enter the verdict** (CLAUDE.md says so explicitly). So the /16
matrix's SRAM cell **is not a latch test**, and the previous draft's promotion
criterion leaned on a diagnostic while never testing the failure mode it feared.

Further, `methodology.md` §8.4 records run-to-run scatter with data held
bit-identical: `ring_osc` **±4 pp against a 5 % gate**; `opamp` **bimodal**.
`sram_snm` and `switchcap` reproduce to **≤0.3 pp and never flip**. **A GPU-vs-CPU
comparison on ring/opamp cells cannot distinguish a flipped basin from the
pipeline's own noise** — so a GPU 16/16 is *not* evidence of fidelity. The
load-bearing evidence must be the deterministic cells plus direct numerical
agreement.

### 8.4 Tiered acceptance for the GPU path — because bit-identity is impossible

**Tier 0 — configuration pin**, fail-fast at import, re-asserted in the harness:

```python
os.environ.setdefault("CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG", ":4096:8")  # before CUDA init
torch.backends.cuda.matmul.allow_tf32 = False   # default False in 2.6 — pin anyway
torch.backends.cudnn.allow_tf32 = False         # default TRUE here — must flip
torch.set_float32_matmul_precision("highest")
torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)
```

Then **assert** each value. TF32 has a 10-bit mantissa → ~1e-3 relative error,
**10× coarser than `RELTOL = 1e-4`** — an outright wrong answer NR will happily
converge to, not amplified noise. Measured in this env: `cuda.matmul.allow_tf32`
is `False` (safe, but the default has moved between torch versions) and
**`cudnn.allow_tf32` is `True` and must be flipped**. **[M]**

**Tier 1 — device-level agreement (no circuit).** GPU arm of
`verify_batched_tail.py` over dense/random/boundary point sets. Criterion:
**≤1e-4 relative** on `id`, `gm`, `gds`, `gmb` and the 5 caps — the precedent
already set by `tests/verify_ar_cache.py`. Separately **publish the
branch-disagreement count** (points where CPU and GPU take different sides of
`_guard_gds` or the sign clamp); per §8 that count, not the relative deviation,
is the physically meaningful number.

**Tier 2 — solved-node agreement.** For every deck in the 16-gate matrix,
compare CPU-pinned and GPU solved node voltages at the OP. Criterion:
`|ΔV| ≤ VNTOL + RELTOL·max(|V_cpu|,|V_gpu|)` — the solver's own convergence
tolerance. Anything outside it means the two paths reached *different* fixed
points and must be listed cell by cell.

**Tier 3 — gate metrics, corrected for §8.2.** Full 16-gate matrix on GPU.
`sram_snm` and `switchcap` are **binding** (≤0.3 pp, no verdict flip);
`ring_osc` and `opamp` are **reported, not binding** (their noise floor exceeds
the gate). Zero FLIPs is binding for all 16. Plus the device suites, including
`verify_nn_lifted_source_dc.py` (the Rule 2 canary must run on any path touching
voltage prep).

> **OMP-strict does not transfer to the GPU.** The strict probe varies
> `PYCIRCUITSIM_TORCH_THREADS ∈ {1,2,4}` because that changes torch's **CPU**
> GEMM reduction order; on CUDA the GEMM is a cuBLAS kernel, so the axis stops
> probing what it was designed to probe. **GPU strict is redefined as:**
> {3 repeat runs} × {device-list order: natural, reversed, seed-shuffled} ×
> {`CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG` unset and `:4096:8`}, with OMP ∈ {1,2,4} retained
> for the CPU tail. Batch composition is the GPU analogue of the OMP axis — and
> since `batch_eval` skips warm devices, group size already varies per NR
> iteration today.

**Tier 4 — the bistable test the suite lacks.** New
`tests/verify_latch_basin_gpu.py`: full cross-coupled 6T cell, `force_ic` from
both stored states, across NFIN corners and all four techs, CPU vs GPU.
**Criterion: the latch settles to the same state in 100 % of cases**, and the
settled node voltages meet Tier 2. A single basin disagreement blocks promotion.
This is the only test that directly probes the failure mode this plan fears.

**Tier 5 — hardware provenance.** Tiers 0–4 establish the path on *one* device.
Reproducibility across GPU models, drivers and cuBLAS versions is **not**
established and must not be claimed. Every GPU number carries
`[gpu: <device>, driver <v>, torch <v>, cuda <v>, tf32=off, det=on]`. Re-run
Tiers 1 and 4 on any hardware or driver change.

## 9. Track B — array-level Monte Carlo (≥100 runs) **[REWRITTEN in rev 3]**

> **Revisions 1–2 designed the wrong thing.** They assumed Track B meant
> *thousands of independent 6T cells* — batched MNA assembly, batched dense LU at
> N≈15, per-circuit NR masking, a new solver class. The confirmed requirement is
> **≥100 runs of a full array** (32×16 … 256×128) with per-device Vt/geometry
> mismatch. Every sample is a **large sparse** system, not a tiny dense one.
> The batched-dense-LU design does not apply and is **withdrawn**; so is the
> "partition samples across 3 GPUs with zero communication" argument, which was
> premised on tiny independent circuits.

### 9.1 What array-level MC actually costs **[rewritten again in rev 4 — transient samples]**

The MC deliverable is a **transient** metric distribution (write margin,
read-disturb, timing), so each sample is a 200-step write/read op, not an
`.op`. Per §2.6t, one 256×128 write op after all phases is ~28 min, of which
~10 min is parse. Parse depends only on the netlist, and MC varies *device
parameters*, not topology. So:

| strategy | 100 transient samples @ 256×128 | vs today |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| today (no phases) | **~7 months** | unusable |
| all phases, re-parsing every sample | ~47 h | — |
| **all phases, parse once + 100 re-runs** | **~30 h** | **1.6× over re-parse** |
| **+ 3 concurrent samples (3 GPUs, §9.2c)** | **~10 h** | overnight ✓ |

**Parse-once is worth 1.6× here** (it was 5.6× on `.op` MC — the transient
sample is longer, so parse amortises less dramatically), and it remains the
cheapest item: parse the netlist once, then for each sample perturb the
per-device parameters in place and re-run. Nothing about the solver, the NN,
or the GPU changes.

**Per marginal transient sample: ~18 min** at 256×128 — stamping (~7 min) +
`other` (~6 min) + solver (~3.6 min) + GPU NN (~1 min). **The per-device
Python is still the majority of a marginal run**, so Phase 2/3b vectorisation
quality directly sets MC throughput; the solver term (620 factorisations ×
100 samples = 62,000 factorisations) is exactly the refactorisation workload
Phase 4b targets.
*(The rev-3 `.op`-sample table — 12.5 days → 3.5 h — described a workload MC
does not run; superseded.)*

### 9.2 What Track B needs built

- **9a — parse once, re-solve N times.** An entry point that parses a netlist,
  then applies a per-sample parameter perturbation to the existing device
  objects and re-solves. The device objects already hold their own mutable
  state (`_eval_cache`, `_q_prev`, …, `mosfet_nn.py:284-292`), so the
  requirement is a **documented reset** between samples — otherwise sample *k*
  inherits sample *k−1*'s cached evaluation and the results are silently
  correlated. This is the single highest-risk correctness item in Track B and
  it does not exist today.
- **9b — per-device mismatch injection.** Vt/geometry mismatch must reach the NN
  input. Geometry (`L`, `NFIN`) is a per-row input column, so it is free; Vt
  enters via the **embedding row** (`local_variant_code`, `parser.py:320`),
  which is *categorical*, not continuous. **Continuous Vt mismatch has no path
  into the NN surface today.** This is a modelling gap, not a performance one,
  and it must be resolved before any yield number is credible — options are a
  continuous Vt input column (requires retraining all checkpoints) or mapping
  mismatch onto geometry. **Flagged as blocking for Track B; out of scope for
  this plan to solve.** **[M]**
- **9c — sample-level parallelism.** 100 independent array runs are
  embarrassingly parallel *across processes*. With ~3 GB of GPU memory per
  256×128 array (§2.5) and 3 GPUs, 3–6 concurrent samples fit comfortably. This
  is process-level scheduling, not batched kernels — far simpler than what
  revisions 1–2 proposed, and it recovers most of the same throughput.
  **3 concurrent samples takes the 3.5 h sweep to ~1.2 h. [E]**

### 9.3 Accuracy guard for array-level MC

The revision-2 guard was written for per-cell SNM distributions and mostly does
not transfer. What does:

- **Determinism between samples.** Re-solving in the same process must give
  bit-identical results to a fresh parse of the same perturbed netlist. This is
  the direct test for the 9a state-reset hazard. **Any difference is a bug, not
  noise** — run it on 10 samples at 32×16 before trusting any larger sweep.
- **Per-sample convergence accounting.** `_solve_dc_with_retry` escalates
  fast path → GMIN → pseudo-transient, and each rung can select a *different
  basin* for a bistable latch. **Report the count of samples reaching each rung.
  A sweep with any pseudo-transient sample is not a yield result** until those
  samples are re-run on the scalar CPU path.
- **A CPU-scalar reference subset.** 5 of the 100 samples, chosen to span the
  metric range plus the two most marginal, re-run with `PYCIRCUITSIM_NN_DEVICE`
  unset and `NN_BATCHED_EVAL=0`. Criterion: solved node voltages agree to
  §8.4 Tier 2 (`|ΔV| ≤ VNTOL + RELTOL·max|V|`), and the latch state matches in
  100 % of cells.
- **The tail still gates, not the mean.** With only 100 samples the tail is
  ~2–3 samples, so quantile criteria are weak. **Report the full sorted sample
  list, not summary statistics**, and treat the worst 3 as individually
  reviewable rather than statistically bounded.

### 9.4 Sequencing

Track B is **not** blocked on the GPU. Ranked by payoff for the transient-MC
workload: **the commit-path batch (2t) → Phase 2 vectorisation → Phase 3 GPU
→ §5.2 ordering (+ 4b refactorisation across 62k factorisations) →
parse-once (9a) → 9c process parallelism.** The mismatch-injection gap (9b)
blocks *credibility*, not performance, and should be resolved first of all.
Note the transient adds one reset hazard to 9a: the per-device charge state
(`_q_prev`, `_q_prev2`, `_i_prev_*`, `_v_prev_tran`) must be re-initialised
between samples or sample *k* integrates from sample *k−1*'s final charges.

Three independent lines of evidence converge on the same routing: the **Amdahl
split** (§2.1) says the solver is not the target; the **A/B on the live GPU

**movement-inclusive round-trip measurement** (§3.2) says the reason is Python
marshalling, not the bus.

> **Revision 3 supersedes this table's framing** (measured at 32×32; at the
> real sizes the `.op` ranking is parse → per-device Python → solver ordering →
> GPU NN). **Revision 4 supersedes it again**: the workload is the
> *transient*, where the ranking becomes **commit-path batch (60–85 %) → GPU
> NN → per-device Python → solver ordering + refactorisation → parse**.
> §2.4t, §2.6t.

The NN is the right GPU target by two orders of magnitude — and on the
transient it is evaluated ~683 times per 200-step op (483 NR iterations +
200 commits), not 41. Four honest headlines:

1. **The transient's dominant cost was invisible to every `.op` profile:**
   the post-step charge-history commit runs N cache-cold batch-1 evals per
   accepted timestep — measured **75–83 % of the whole transient**. Batching
   it (2t) is ~10 lines and the single biggest win in the plan; the GPU then
   carries the same batched call.
2. **The bit-identical win is still real but re-scoped**: Phases 1 + 2 are
   worth ~6.6× on the NR loop of a transient (not the 1.71× end-to-end that
   `.op` arithmetic suggested), with no GPU, no re-gate, no accuracy risk,
   under §8.1's two constraints.
3. **The solver matters again on this workload**: ~620 factorisations per op
   and 3×10⁴ per characterisation run. MMD ordering is the one-parameter 76×;
   KLU-class refactorisation is re-promoted for ≥64×32 transients.
4. **Parse no longer leads, but it closes the loop**: 1–8 % of a transient
   today, ~30 % after everything else lands, and parse-once still gates MC.
